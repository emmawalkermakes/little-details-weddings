The wedding is over, the flowers have wilted, and there's a stack of cards and gifts on the dining table. Somewhere between the honeymoon and going back to work, you're meant to thank everyone personally. For a lot of couples, this is the part of wedding planning nobody warned them about.

The good news: thank-you cards don't need to be long or perfect. They need to be personal, and they need to actually get sent. This guide is about making both of those things easy.

## Start before the wedding, not after

The single biggest time-saver is a gift list that you start before the day. Set up a simple spreadsheet or a page in a notebook with these columns:

- Guest name(s)
- Postal address
- Gift received (with a short description)
- Date received
- Card written
- Card posted

Collect addresses when you send invitations, since you'll need them again. If your registry is online, many registry services let you download a list of who bought what. Add those entries as they come in.

On the day itself, ask a trusted person, such as a sibling or close friend, to collect cards and gifts from the gift table and keep them together. A simple trick: as you open each card, tape it or clip it to the gift it came with, or write the giver's name on the box straight away. Envelopes full of cash and gift cards without names are how couples end up guessing.

## When to send them

There's no law here, and etiquette guides vary. A common rule of thumb is to send thank-you cards within about two to three months of the wedding. For gifts that arrive before the wedding, try to thank people within a few weeks of receiving them, while it's fresh.

What matters more than the exact date is not letting it drift for a year. If you are running late, send them anyway. A late, warm card is much better than none.

## A simple structure for every card

Most good thank-you notes follow the same four short beats:

1. **Greeting.** Use the name you'd call them in person.
2. **Thank them for the specific gift.** Name it, so it doesn't read like a form letter.
3. **Say how you'll use it or what it meant.** One sentence is plenty.
4. **Mention them being there (or being missed), and sign off.**

That's three to five sentences. You don't need more.

## Sample wording for common situations

Feel free to borrow these and change them to sound like you.

**A registry gift**
> Dear Sue and Graham, thank you so much for the cast-iron pot. We've already used it twice, and Sam has decided he's now a "slow-cooking person". It meant a lot to have you both there with us on the day. Love, Jess and Sam.

**Cash or a gift card**
It's fine to mention money; just be warm about it rather than naming the amount.
> Dear Uncle Tony, thank you for your generous gift. We're putting it towards our first trip away together as a married couple, and we'll send you a photo from the beach. Thank you for making the drive up for the day.

**A handmade or sentimental gift**
> Dear Nan, thank you for the quilt. We know how many hours went into it, and it's already on our bed. We'll treasure it, just like we treasured having you there to see us married.

**A guest who couldn't attend**
> Dear Priya, thank you for the beautiful vase and your lovely card. We missed you on the day, and we can't wait to catch up properly when you're back in Brisbane.

**Your wedding party or helpers**
These deserve a little more than a standard card. Mention something specific they did.
> Dear Mel, thank you for everything: the dress steaming, the speech that made Dad cry, and fixing the sign that blew over at the ceremony. We couldn't have done it without you.

**A group gift**
Send one card to the group organiser and, if you can, a short note to each person who chipped in. If that's not practical, a card to the group addressed with everyone's names is fine.

## Keeping it manageable

The trick is to break it into small sessions instead of a single marathon.

- **Do five a night.** Twenty minutes, a cup of tea, then stop. A list of a hundred guests is done in a few weeks.
- **Split them between you.** Each partner writes to their own side of the family and friends, and both sign every card.
- **Address envelopes first.** It's the boring part, so get it out of the way in one sitting, or ask a helper with neat handwriting.
- **Buy stamps in bulk.** Check the current letter rates on the Australia Post website and buy enough for every card plus a few spares.
- **Tick off as you go.** Update your spreadsheet as each card is posted, so nobody gets missed or thanked twice.

## Choosing the cards

You can use plain cards, a set that matches your invitations, or cards with a favourite photo from the day. Photo cards are a lovely touch, but they depend on your photographer's delivery timeline, which can take weeks or months. If you want them, consider a short thank-you note or message to close family first, and the photo cards later.

Leave enough blank space to write by hand. Even a printed card should have a personal, handwritten line or two. That's what people keep.

## Is a text or email enough?

For a quick acknowledgement that a gift arrived safely, especially one that was posted, a short message is thoughtful. Most people still appreciate a written card afterwards, particularly older relatives. If you know a friend genuinely prefers digital, a personal email is fine. Go with what will feel personal to the person receiving it.

## One last thing

Thank-you cards are also a chance to look back at the day properly. Many couples say that writing them is when it really sinks in how many people showed up for them. Put on some music, open the gift list, and enjoy remembering who was there.
