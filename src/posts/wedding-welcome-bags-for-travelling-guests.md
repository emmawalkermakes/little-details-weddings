Somewhere around the three-month mark, most couples hosting guests from out of town have the same thought: *should we be doing welcome bags?*

You've probably seen them online — tote bags overflowing with snacks, mini bottles, local goodies and a beautifully printed itinerary. They look lovely. They're also one more job in a season that already has plenty.

This post is about deciding honestly whether they're right for your wedding, and if they are, making them useful rather than decorative.

## Start with one question: who's actually travelling?

Welcome bags make the most sense for guests who've come a long way and are staying somewhere unfamiliar. Think interstate family flying into Brisbane for a weekend at the coast, overseas friends landing a few days early, or a whole group booked into the same resort or holiday houses near a regional venue.

They make far less sense if nearly everyone lives within an hour's drive and is going home to their own bed.

A quick way to check: open your guest list and highlight everyone who'll need accommodation. If it's a small group, a welcome bag is a manageable, warm gesture. If it's most of the list, consider whether a simpler version (see below) gets you the same goodwill for a fraction of the effort.

It is also completely fine not to do them. Guests don't expect them, and nobody has ever left a wedding disappointed by their absence.

## What's genuinely useful (in an Australian summer)

The best welcome bags solve small problems a visitor has on day one. If you're marrying in Queensland or anywhere warm between November and March, that mostly means heat, hydration and "where do I go?"

**Water.** A bottle of water is the most-used item in any bag. Guests arrive after a flight or a long drive, often into a warm room.

**Sun protection.** A small sunscreen is genuinely handy for overseas guests who may not realise how strong the Australian sun is. Lip balm with SPF is a nice extra.

**Something salty and something sweet.** Pick snacks that won't melt. Chocolate is a gamble in a hot car or an unairconditioned room; muesli bars, crackers, nuts, dried fruit and individually wrapped biscuits are safer. Check your guest list for allergies, and avoid nuts if you're unsure.

**A local touch.** A small treat made or grown in your area tells guests something about where they are. Keep it to one or two things rather than a hamper.

**Pain relief and an electrolyte sachet** — sometimes jokingly called the "day after" kit. Plenty of couples include these. Keep everything sealed and in its original packaging.

**Insect repellent**, if you're near water, rainforest or holding anything outdoors around dusk.

Things that sound nice but often go unused: candles, full-size toiletries, heavy glass items, and anything guests then have to fit into carry-on luggage on the way home.

## The part guests read twice: the note

If you only do one thing, do this. A single printed card (A5 or smaller works well) with the information travelling guests keep asking you for:

- **Timings** for each event, including any welcome drinks or recovery breakfast
- **Addresses** of the ceremony and reception, with a note on parking or drop-off
- **Transport**: shuttle times, local taxi or rideshare notes, or who's driving whom
- **Dress and weather hints**: "The ceremony is on grass — block heels are your friend," or "Bring a light layer, the evening breeze can be cool."
- **A contact number** that isn't yours. Give guests a trusted friend or family member to call on the day, so your phone stays in your bag.
- **Two or three local suggestions**: a café for breakfast, a walk, a beach or a lookout. Places you actually like, not a tourist-brochure list.

Write the note in your own voice. A line or two of genuine thanks — "We know you've come a long way, and it means a lot" — is what people remember.

Double-check every time and address before you print. If anything could change (shuttle times are the usual culprit), add "Final times will be texted on the day" rather than printing something that ends up wrong.

## How to get them to guests

The logistics trip people up more than the contents. Options, roughly from easiest to hardest:

**1. Hotel or resort front desk.** Many accommodation providers will hand bags to guests at check-in. Ask early, ask what they need from you (usually a list of guest names and booking names), and check whether there's a handling fee. Label each bag clearly with the name the booking is under — not a nickname.

**2. A family member drops them off.** Works well for holiday houses or Airbnbs, as long as you've arranged access with the hosts and the person delivering has a list.

**3. Hand them out at a welcome event.** If you're hosting drinks or a barbecue the night before, put the bags by the door and let guests grab one. Low-stress, and you get to see their faces.

**4. Post them ahead.** Possible for small, flat items (a card and a few treats), but more fiddly and less reliable. Usually only worth it for very small numbers.

Whatever you choose, give one person the job of owning delivery so it isn't on your list on the wedding-week Thursday.

## Make it simple: three levels

**The minimum.** A printed welcome note and a bottle of water, left with the hotel. That's it. Guests feel looked after and you've spent very little time.

**The middle ground.** Note, water, sunscreen, two or three snacks, one local treat, in a paper bag or small tote with a name tag.

**The full welcome.** Everything above, plus a little personalised element — a tote or tag with your names and date, or a printed map of the area marked with your favourite spots.

Pick a level that matches your budget and, honestly, your energy. A well-thought-out minimum beats an overstuffed bag assembled at midnight.

## A packing-night plan

If you're assembling them yourselves, do it in one sitting, two to four weeks out (keep anything perishable for closer to the date):

- Set up a production line on a big table, one item type per pile.
- Pack everything except the perishable snacks and drinks.
- Store finished bags somewhere cool and dry — not a hot garage in a Queensland summer.
- In the final week, add the remaining items and tag each bag with a guest name.
- Pack the tagged bags into labelled boxes by hotel or address, with the list taped to the lid.

Rope in your bridal party or a couple of friends, put on some music and order pizza. It's one of the few wedding jobs that's actually fun with a group.

## The bottom line

A welcome bag isn't about the stuff. It's a way of saying "thanks for coming all this way, here's what you need to know". If a printed note and a cold bottle of water says that for you, you've done it well.
