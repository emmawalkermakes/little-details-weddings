Most couples want a ceremony that sounds like them, not like a template. The good news is that in Australia almost all of a civil ceremony is yours to shape. There are just a few legal pieces that have to be there, and knowing exactly what they are makes the personalising much easier.

This is a plain-English Q&A based on guidance from the Attorney-General's Department and the wording in the *Marriage Act 1961*. It's general information, not legal advice. Your authorised celebrant is the person to confirm the details for your wedding, and rules can change, so check the [Attorney-General's Department "Get married" page](https://www.ag.gov.au/families-and-marriage/marriage/get-married) for the current version.

## Before the day

### What paperwork has to happen first?

You need to complete a **Notice of Intended Marriage (NOIM)** and give it to your authorised celebrant **at least one month and no more than 18 months** before the wedding. Your celebrant will also need evidence of your date and place of birth (a birth certificate or passport), identification, and proof that any previous marriage has ended.

Since changes to the Marriage Act in June 2024, a NOIM can be witnessed remotely by audio-visual link as well as in person. Your celebrant must also meet separately and in person with each of you before the wedding.

### We've left it late. Is a month really the minimum?

Generally, yes. If there's less than a month to go, talk to your celebrant. A prescribed authority can approve a shortened notice period in some circumstances. The department lists reasons such as employment or travel commitments, wedding or celebration arrangements, medical reasons, legal proceedings and an error in giving notice. It's an approval, not an automatic right, so don't plan around it.

If you're hoping to marry in the new year and haven't lodged a NOIM yet, put it on your list now. The Christmas and New Year break is a busy time for celebrants, and it's easy to lose a couple of weeks to holidays.

## On the day: the legal parts

### Who has to be there?

At minimum: the two of you, your authorised celebrant (who must be physically present), and **two witnesses aged over 18**. Everyone else is optional, which is why some couples choose a tiny legal ceremony and a bigger celebration later.

### What does the celebrant have to say?

In a civil ceremony, the celebrant must say what's known as the **monitum**, or words to that effect. The standard wording is:

> "I am duly authorised by law to solemnise marriages according to law. Before you are joined in marriage in my presence and in the presence of these witnesses, I am to remind you of the solemn and binding nature of the relationship into which you are now about to enter. Marriage, according to law in Australia, is the union of two people to the exclusion of all others, voluntarily entered into for life."

You don't write this part, but you can ask your celebrant where in the ceremony it sits. Many celebrants weave it in naturally so it doesn't feel like a sudden switch into legal language.

### What do we have to say?

Each of you must say the legal vows, or words to that effect:

> "I call upon the persons here present to witness that I, [your name], take thee, [partner's name], to be my lawful wedded wife / husband / spouse."

This is the moment you are legally married, so it isn't the place to get creative with the core structure.

### Can we change those vow words at all?

A little. [One Australian celebrant association's guide to the legal words](https://www.celebrants.org.au/find/important-things-to-know/the-legal-words-for-a-marriage-ceremony) lists commonly accepted swaps, including:

- "call upon" can become "ask"
- "persons" can become "people"
- "thee" can become "you"
- "persons here present" can become "everyone here" or "everybody here"
- "wife", "husband" or "spouse" can become "partner in marriage"
- you can leave out either "lawful" or "wedded", but not both

One example it gives as acceptable: "I ask everyone here to witness that I, A.B., take you, C.D., to be my wedded wife (husband or spouse)."

Some changes aren't accepted. For example, "family and friends" can't replace "persons here present". If you want a variation, run the exact wording past your celebrant well before the day.

### Then can we add our own vows?

Yes. You can say as much as you like **after** the legal words, as long as nothing you add contradicts them. Many couples say the legal line first, then read personal vows. Others put their personal vows earlier and keep the legal line short and clear. Either works.

### What gets signed?

Three marriage certificates are signed on the day by the two of you, your celebrant and your two witnesses. You'll be given a ceremonial certificate on the day. Your celebrant then lodges the paperwork with the registry of births, deaths and marriages in your state or territory within 14 days.

The ceremonial certificate is a keepsake. If you plan to change your name or need to prove you're married, apply to the registry for the official certificate. Your celebrant may be able to apply for you, generally for a fee.

## The parts that are yours

### What can we personalise?

Nearly everything else. A few ideas that make a ceremony feel personal without making it longer:

- **The welcome.** A sentence or two about why you've chosen this place, or a nod to family who couldn't travel.
- **Your story.** Ask your celebrant to tell it briefly, with one or two specific details rather than a full biography.
- **Readings.** A poem, a song lyric, a passage from a favourite book, or a letter from a grandparent read aloud.
- **A ritual.** Handfasting, a ring warming (where guests hold the rings for a moment), or a cultural tradition from either family.
- **Who stands up.** A friend can do a reading, a sibling can bring the rings, and parents can be asked for their blessing.
- **The music.** Walking in, signing and walking out are three natural music moments.

### Can a friend run the whole ceremony?

A friend or family member can host parts of it, such as the welcome, your story or a reading. But the legal parts must be done by an authorised celebrant who is physically present. Some couples have their celebrant handle the legal sections while a friend leads the rest. Talk to your celebrant about how they like to share the floor.

### Can it be a surprise for one of us?

No. Both people must freely agree to marry, and celebrants won't solemnise a surprise wedding where one person doesn't know it's happening. A surprise for your *guests* is a different matter. A party that turns out to be a wedding is fine, as long as the paperwork and notice period have been done properly.

### We married overseas. Can we have a "legal" ceremony here too?

An overseas marriage is generally recognised in Australia if it was valid where it happened and would have been valid here. A celebrant can't present a second ceremony as a legal marriage, but you can absolutely hold a commitment or celebration ceremony.

## A simple ceremony-planning checklist

- [ ] Book your authorised celebrant
- [ ] Lodge your NOIM (at least one month, no more than 18 months ahead)
- [ ] Gather birth and ID documents, plus divorce or death certificates if relevant
- [ ] Each meet your celebrant separately and in person
- [ ] Choose two witnesses aged over 18
- [ ] Confirm the exact wording of your legal vows with your celebrant
- [ ] Write personal vows, readings and the story section
- [ ] Choose music for walking in, signing and walking out
- [ ] Decide who holds the rings and who reads
- [ ] Order your official marriage certificate after the wedding if you'll need it

Get the legal pieces sorted early, and the rest of the ceremony becomes the fun part.
