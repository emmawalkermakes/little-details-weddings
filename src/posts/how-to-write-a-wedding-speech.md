Being asked to give a speech at a wedding is a compliment. It's also, for plenty of people, a source of low-level dread from the moment they say yes.

The good news is that a wedding audience is about the friendliest crowd you'll ever face. Nobody wants you to fail. They want to laugh a little, maybe tear up a little, and raise a glass. You don't need to be funny or polished. You need to be warm, clear and reasonably brief.

This guide works whether you're the best man, maid of honour, a parent, a sibling, a friend, or one half of the couple. Australian weddings have moved well beyond the traditional line-up of speakers, so the structure here is deliberately flexible.

## Start with three questions

Before you write a single line, get answers to these from the couple or the MC:

1. **How long should I speak?** Ask for a number. If no one gives you one, aim for about three to five minutes. That's usually somewhere around 400 to 700 words spoken at a relaxed pace. Shorter is almost always better than longer.
2. **Where do I sit in the running order?** If you're speaking after three others, you'll want to avoid repeating the same stories, and you might go shorter.
3. **Is there anything off-limits?** Some couples are happy for anything to be shared. Others would rather not hear about exes, a past job, a health matter or family tension. Ask directly, and respect the answer.

## A five-part structure that works for almost anyone

### 1. Hello and who you are

One or two sentences. Not everyone in the room knows you.

> "Hi everyone, I'm Sam. I've been Priya's friend since we were eleven and sat next to each other in Year 7 science."

### 2. Thank-yous (keep it short)

If you're a parent or one of the couple, this is where you might thank guests for coming, people who travelled, and those who helped. If you're a best man or bridesmaid, a quick thank-you to the couple for including you is plenty. Don't read out a long list; it loses the room.

### 3. One or two stories

This is the heart of the speech. Pick **one or two specific stories** rather than a long list of adjectives. "She's kind and loyal" is forgettable. A short story that *shows* her kindness is not.

Good stories for a wedding speech:

- show something true and good about the person or the couple
- can be understood by everyone, including grandparents and the plus-ones who've never met them
- end on a warm note, even if they start with a laugh

Avoid long in-jokes, anything that would embarrass the couple in front of family, and stories that are mostly about you.

### 4. The couple together

Turn from the person you know best to the two of them. What changed when they met? What do you see in them as a pair? A small, specific observation works better than a big general claim.

> "The first time I saw Jordan quietly refill Priya's water bottle before she'd even noticed it was empty, I thought, oh, this one's paying attention."

### 5. The toast

Tell people what's about to happen so they can pick up their glasses, then keep the toast itself short and clear.

> "If you could all please stand and raise your glasses. To Priya and Jordan."

That's it. Sit down, and enjoy the rest of the night.

## Writing it: tips that make a real difference

**Write for the ear, not the page.** Use short sentences. Read every line out loud as you write it. If you stumble over a phrase at your kitchen bench, you'll stumble over it on the night.

**Borrow quotes sparingly.** A line from a poem or song can be lovely, but one is enough, and your own words almost always land better than something from the internet.

**Check every name.** Pronunciation and spelling of names matter, especially for family members you don't know well.

**Cut ruthlessly.** When you think you're finished, take out the weakest story or paragraph. Your speech will be better for it.

## Practise, then practise standing up

Read it aloud at least five or six times before the day, and at least once standing up, holding whatever you'll hold on the night. Time yourself. Most people speak faster when nervous, but they also pause for laughs, so the timing tends to even out.

If you can, practise once in front of someone who'll be honest with you and isn't one of the couple.

You don't need to memorise it. Most wedding guests are perfectly happy to see someone reading from notes.

## Cards, paper or phone?

- **Small cards** are easy to hold and look tidy. Number them, in case you drop them.
- **A single folded sheet** in large print works fine too.
- **A phone** is convenient, but turn on Do Not Disturb, set the screen so it won't dim or lock, and bump up the text size. A notification popping up mid-sentence is distracting.

Whatever you use, print or set the text large enough to read in dim reception lighting.

## On the night

**Go easy on the drinks before your speech.** One to settle nerves is a personal call. Several is a risk you don't need to take.

**Use the microphone properly.** Hold it a hand's width or so from your mouth and keep it there, even when you look around the room. If there's a test moment before speeches, take it.

**Look up.** Glance at the couple during the stories and the toast. They're the reason you're there.

**If you lose your place,** pause, take a breath, find the line, and carry on. Guests rarely notice a pause. They notice panic.

**If you get emotional,** that's fine. Stop, smile, take a sip of water. It's a wedding.

## A quick note for couples

If you're planning speeches for your own wedding, a few things help your speakers enormously:

- Tell them the time limit and the running order well in advance.
- Let the MC know who's speaking and how to pronounce their names.
- Put speeches at a time when guests are seated and attentive, and before the dance floor opens.
- Check that the venue has a working microphone and that it reaches where speakers will stand.

A good speech isn't about being clever. It's about saying, in front of the people who matter most, that you're glad these two found each other. Keep that in mind, keep it short, and you'll be fine.
